Course learning outcome

"Apply a problem-solving process to routine transactions and events related to assets, revenue and their related accounts to assess the recognition, measurement and subsequent measurement of these financial statement elements under IFRS and ASPE."

A student comment featured directly in the Week 1 deck: "The PBL sessions [were] very helpful as the process was replicated in the assessments (i.e. midterms and/or final exam)." — in other words, the way you practice weekly is the way you'll be tested. Treat every PBL scenario as exam rehearsal.

Why IFRS and ASPE, always

  • IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards) — required for publicly accountable enterprises (publicly traded companies, or private companies that need IFRS for a specific reason, e.g. a bank covenant requires it, or the company is planning to go public in the near term).
  • ASPE (Accounting Standards for Private Enterprises) — the default option for private Canadian companies that don't need IFRS.
  • Almost every PBL and exam question requires you to first determine which standard applies (a fact usually embedded in the case background), then apply that standard's specific criteria — this is why "Assess the Situation" step 3(a) of the Problem-Solving Process exists.

Recurring theoretical themes (Week 1)

Information asymmetry

A condition where some people have more information than others. Accounting exists to transmit information from those who have it to those who need it — "better information leads to better economic decisions."

Conceptual Framework — Who / How / What

Who are the users (existing/potential investors, lenders, other creditors)? How do they use it (making resource-allocation decisions)? What do they need (amount, timing and risk/uncertainty of future cash flows)?

Non-GAAP measures

Numbers like EBITDA, Adjusted EBIT, and Free Cash Flow reported outside the financial statements (press releases, MD&A) — not defined by IFRS or ASPE, so comparability across companies is limited.

Earnings management

"The active manipulation of accounting information to create an altered impression of the firm's financial performance, as measured by its earnings" — exploits the judgement and estimates inherent in accruals and deferrals.

Never skip a stage, and never analyze before you've clearly identified what the issue is — a common way to lose marks is jumping straight to a journal entry without stating why it's an issue.
1
Assess the Situation
  • Read the information carefully — scan first, then do a detailed reading.
  • Identify the users of the financial statements and their objectives: Who are the key users? Who requested the information? What have they specifically asked you to do?
  • Identify contextual factors: Is the entity publicly traded (IFRS) or private (ASPE, unless IFRS is required or appropriate — e.g. a bank requires it, or the company is going public soon)? What's the entity's industry and business model? Are there circumstances that might bias the decision-maker toward a preferred treatment (e.g. management wants to hit an earnings target)? What's material?
  • Identify the relevant past and planned future transactions/events and build a timeline.
2
Identify the Issue(s) Explain what the financial reporting issue(s) is, and why you think it's an issue, using specific case facts — not generic textbook language. Tie every issue back to something the case actually said.
3
Analyze the Issue(s)
  • Determine whether a relevant accounting standard applies to support your analysis — if so, also consider whether the Conceptual Framework adds useful support.
  • If no standard applies, apply the Conceptual Framework directly.
  • For each issue, apply the relevant standard(s) (specific standard and/or Conceptual Framework) to the specific case facts — where applicable, argue both sides.
  • Identify where estimates and/or judgement are required.
  • Reality-check your analysis against the case context — does it make sense? Does it tie back to the objective identified in Step 1?
4
Conclude, Justify and Recommend
  • Think before you write.
  • For each issue, clearly and concisely state your specific financial reporting choice and document your rationale.
  • Consider the impact on the Balance Sheet (Statement of Financial Position) and the Income Statement.
  • Recommend the appropriate journal and/or correcting entries.
Critical distinctionConclude is a comment — a statement of your accounting position and why. Recommend is an action — the specific journal entry, disclosure, or presentation change that follows from your conclusion. Exam graders are looking for both, explicitly.

How to use this on every PBL and exam question

  1. Before writing anything, scan the whole case once, then re-read it slowly and note the entity's IFRS/ASPE status, industry, and any hints of management bias or a specific ask from a supervisor/user.
  2. List every financial reporting issue you can find — revenue timing? asset vs. expense? a provision vs. a contingency? — and for each, write one sentence connecting it to specific case facts.
  3. For each issue, walk the relevant standard's criteria one at a time against the case facts (this is exactly the "Criteria | Met?" table format used throughout the lecture slides — reproduce that structure in your own answer).
  4. Finish every issue with an explicit conclusion sentence and the resulting journal entry — don't leave the reader to infer your position from the numbers alone.
Week 1

Conceptual Framework & Financial Reporting Theory

The theoretical foundation everything else builds on: who financial statements are for, what makes information useful, and how IFRS and ASPE frame the same questions slightly differently.

Information asymmetry
Information asymmetryA condition in which some people have more information than others. Accounting is the production of information about an enterprise and the transmission of that information from those who have it to those who need it (Lo & Fisher).

"Better information leads to better economic decisions." The recurring "buying a used car" analogy: buyers want relevant and reliable information to support decisions about allocating capital (loaning funds, investing).

Purpose of the Conceptual Framework
IFRS SP1.1–1.2The Conceptual Framework exists to: (a) assist the IASB in developing Standards based on consistent concepts; (b) assist preparers in developing consistent accounting policies when no Standard applies, or when a Standard allows a choice; and (c) assist all parties in understanding and interpreting the Standards. "The Conceptual Framework is not a Standard. Nothing in the Conceptual Framework overrides any Standard."
Non-GAAP financial measures
  • Financial statements themselves follow GAAP (IFRS or ASPE) — e.g. Net Income on the Income Statement.
  • Non-GAAP measures live outside the financial statements — press releases, MD&A — e.g. EBITDA, Adjusted EBIT, Free Cash Flow.
  • The Conceptual Framework explicitly recognizes this limitation: general-purpose financial reports "do not and cannot provide all of the information that existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors need" — users must also consider other sources (economic conditions, political climate, industry outlook).
  • IFRS 18 (new standard on Presentation and Disclosure in Financial Statements) responds to concerns about: variety in P&L structure/subtotals/content; lack of understanding of how management-defined performance measures are calculated and why they're used; and lack of clarity on what belongs on the face of the statements vs. in the notes. It introduces new defined categories and subtotals in the P&L (referenced again in Weeks 7–8 and 13 for cash flow statement placement of interest/dividends).
Earnings management
Earnings management"The active manipulation of accounting information to create an altered impression of the firm's financial performance, as measured by its earnings."

Operates through judgement and estimates embedded in accruals and deferrals — the same accruals/deferrals that, applied in an unbiased way, are simply normal accounting, become earnings management when applied with an excessive or insufficient bias. This is exactly why "Assess the Situation" asks you to identify circumstances that might bias a decision-maker toward a preferred accounting treatment.

Week 2

Revenue Recognition I — The IFRS 15 Five-Step Model

Why revenue is the single most exam-relevant topic in this course, and the full five-step model with worked examples for each step.

Why revenue is important
  • Common to almost all entities and a significant number on the financial statements.
  • Key metric for net income; the basis for EPS; revenue growth is a KPI for almost every entity.
  • Judgement is required in many revenue recognition scenarios — which means it's subject to manipulation (ties directly back to Week 1's earnings management).
Revenue vs. GainsIFRS 15: revenue is income arising in the course of an entity's ordinary activities. ASPE 1000: increases in economic resources from inflows/enhancements of assets or reductions of liabilities, resulting from the entity's ordinary activities.

Example: Company A (a land developer) sells land as its ordinary business → revenue. Company B (a security-systems manufacturer) sells land it held for capital appreciation → gain on sale. Same transaction type, different classification, because "ordinary activities" differs by entity.

IFRS 15's stated objective: "to establish the principles that an entity shall apply to report useful information to users of financial statements about the nature, amount, timing and uncertainty of revenue and cash flows arising from a contract with a customer."
ASPE's alternative approach (no 5-step model)

ASPE does not use the 5-step model. Instead, ASPE recognizes revenue when all of the following are met:

  • Performed — including evidence of an arrangement
  • Measurable — the amount can be reliably measured
  • Collection reasonably assured
  • Risks and rewards transferred (vs. IFRS's "transfer of control")
Exam angleIFRS's core concept is transfer of control; ASPE's is transfer of risks and rewards. These usually — but not always — happen at the same point, so a case can present a scenario where the two frameworks reach different conclusions on timing.
Step 1 — Identify the Contract

All five criteria must be met:

  • Contract has been approved (in writing, orally, or per customary business practice)
  • The rights regarding goods/services to be transferred can be identified
  • The payment terms are specified
  • The contract has commercial substance
  • Collection of consideration is probable
Step 2 — Identify the Performance Obligation(s)

A promised good/service is a separate performance obligation (PO) only if it is distinct — both tests below must be met:

TestQuestion
Capable of being distinctCan the customer benefit from the good/service on its own, or together with other readily available resources? (Watch for significant integration.)
Distinct within the context of the contractIs the promise separately identifiable from other promises in the contract? (Watch for modification/customization, or highly interdependent/interrelated items.)

For AFM 291 purposes, analyze "separately identifiable" by considering performance risks and/or inputs and outputs.

Worked example — Equipment Ltd.Sells a packaging machine (normal price $750,000) and a forklift truck (normal price $325,000) for a combined $1,000,000. Delivered on different dates. Both capable of being distinct (each usable on its own) and distinct within the context of the contract (no significant integration/customization between a packaging machine and a forklift). Conclusion: 2 performance obligations.
Worked example — Hospital construction contractA contractor manages a full hospital build: engineering, site clearance, foundation, procurement, construction, piping/wiring, equipment installation, finishing. Each item is capable of being distinct on its own, but they are highly interdependent and integrated into one overall deliverable (a functioning hospital) — not distinct within the context of the contract. Conclusion: 1 performance obligation. This is the same reasoning applied to Sun Technology Inc.'s solar panel contracts (see the Case Deep-Dive).
Step 3 — Determine the Transaction Price

IFRS 15 par. 48: consider the effects of variable consideration, constraining estimates of variable consideration, significant financing components, non-cash consideration, and consideration payable to a customer.

Variable consideration — two estimation methods

MethodWhen to use
Expected valueProbability-weighted — appropriate for a large number of outcomes with similar characteristics
Most likely amountAppropriate when there are only two possible outcomes (e.g. a contract is either achieved or not achieved)

Principle: estimate the consideration the entity expects to be entitled to — do not artificially overstate revenue at the time of sale.

Significant financing component — worked example (Barnett Corp.)

Barnett sells a machine for $20,000 cash down + four annual instalments of $40,000 (total cash = $180,000), at an appropriate annual interest rate of 9%. IFRS 15 par. 61: adjust the promised consideration so revenue reflects the cash selling price — what the customer would have paid if paying cash at the point of transfer.

Transaction Price: Deposit $ 20,000.00 Deferred payments (PV of $40,000, n=4, i=9%) 129,588.80 Cash-equivalent price (= Revenue) $149,588.80 Jan 1, 2025 journal entry: Cash 20,000.00 Long-term Note Receivable 129,588.80 Revenue 149,588.80 (Cash $180,000 total = Revenue $149,588.80 + Interest Income $30,411.20, recognized over the 4 years using the effective interest method.)

ASPE permits straight-line amortization of the financing component as an alternative to the effective interest method; IFRS requires the effective interest method.

Step 5 — Recognize Revenue

For each performance obligation, determine whether it's satisfied over time or at a point in time.

Over-time criteria (IFRS 15 par. 35) — meet ANY ONE:

  • The customer simultaneously receives and consumes the benefits as the entity performs (test: would another entity need to substantially re-perform the work completed to date to fulfill the remaining obligation?)
  • The entity's performance creates or enhances an asset (e.g. work in progress) that the customer controls as it's created/enhanced
  • The entity's performance does not create an asset with an alternative use to the entity, and the entity has an enforceable right to payment for performance completed to date

Point-in-time indicators (IFRS 15 par. 38) — if none of the above apply:

  • The entity has a present right to payment for the asset
  • The customer has legal title to the asset
  • The entity has transferred physical possession of the asset
  • The customer has the significant risks and rewards of ownership
  • The customer has accepted the asset
Exam angleCustom/customized products built specifically for one client with no resale value to the seller very often satisfy the "no alternative use + enforceable right to payment" over-time test — this is the exact reasoning that applies to Sun Technology Inc.'s custom solar panel arrays.
Week 3

Revenue Recognition II — Warranties & Over-Time Measurement Methods

Assurance-type warranties, series of distinct goods/services, ASPE's percentage-of-completion vs. completed-contract choice, and the input vs. output methods for measuring progress.

Warranties — Assurance-type
Assurance warrantyA warranty that simply provides the customer assurance that the good complies with agreed-upon specifications. This is not a separate performance obligation — instead, it's accounted for as a provision (a warranty liability/expense), consistent with IAS 37 (covered in Week 4).

Contrast with a service-type warranty (providing a service beyond basic assurance of specification compliance), which is a separate performance obligation with its own allocated transaction price — flagged in the deck but not the current course focus.

IFRS 15 — Series of distinct goods or services

A series of distinct goods or services that are substantially the same and have the same pattern of transfer to the customer can be treated as a single performance obligation satisfied over time — this is the basis for the KTI/Polarian shipping-services example below (36 months of substantially identical delivery services = one over-time PO).

ASPE — Percentage of completion vs. completed contract

Where performance is over time under ASPE's "performed" criterion, ASPE offers a choice between two methods:

MethodApproach
Percentage of completionRecognize revenue/profit progressively as work is performed — used when the entity can reasonably estimate progress toward completion.
Completed contractRecognize revenue/profit only when the contract is substantially complete — used when the entity cannot reasonably estimate progress.
Measuring progress — Input vs. Output methods
MethodBasis
Input methodCosts incurred to date ÷ total expected costs — measures the entity's effort/inputs toward satisfying the obligation. Most common in this course (KTI/Polarian, Sun Technology).
Output methodDirectly measures the value transferred to the customer — e.g. surveys of performance completed, appraisals of results achieved, milestones reached, units produced/delivered.
Exam angleA cost-overrun (actual costs exceeding budget) inflates the input-method percentage-complete without necessarily reflecting more value delivered to the customer — this is a common discussion point: does the input method still faithfully represent the transfer of control, or would an output method (e.g. based on units/buildings actually completed and functional) better depict performance? This exact question is asked directly in the Sun Technology Inc. case.
Worked example — Path Pavers (output method)

Where a contract's progress can be reliably measured by physical output (e.g. kilometres of road paved, square footage completed) rather than cost inputs, the output method directly ties revenue recognized to the proportion of the physical job completed — bypassing any distortion from cost efficiency or inefficiency during the period.

Onerous contracts
Onerous contract (IAS 37)"A contract in which the unavoidable cost of meeting the obligations under the contract exceeds the economic benefits expected to be received under it." The unavoidable cost is the lower of the cost of fulfilling the contract and any compensation/penalties for failing to fulfil it. If onerous, "the present obligation under the contract shall be recognised and measured as a provision."
KTI/Polarian, end of 20X2: Fixed-price contract 9,100,000 Total expected costs (5,500,000 to date + 3,870,000 to complete) (9,370,000) Expected loss on ENTIRE contract (270,000) Gross loss already recognized to date (158,485) Additional expected loss = provision needed 111,515 Expected Loss / Cost of Sales 111,515 Provision for Onerous Contract 111,515
ASPE's parallel guidance on expected losses"When it is probable that total contract costs will exceed total contract revenue, the entire expected loss is recognized as an expense immediately" — regardless of whether work has commenced, or the degree of completion.

Note: the onerous-contract provision itself must still satisfy the three provision-recognition criteria below (present obligation, probable outflow, reliable estimate) — it's simply a specific application of IAS 37's general provisions guidance.

Provisions vs. Contingent Liabilities
Provisions

Meet the Conceptual Framework's definition of a liability → recognized on the Statement of Financial Position.

Contingent Liabilities

Do not meet full recognition criteria → not recognized on the SFP; disclosed in notes only (unless remote).

IAS 37 decision tree — liabilities
Reproduce this exact "Criteria | Met?" logic in your written answers: (1) present obligation from an obligating event, (2) probable outflow of economic resources, (3) reliable estimate — all three met = provision; some met = contingent liability disclosure; none/remote = nothing.

Worked example — Contaminated land (constructive obligation)

An oil company causes contamination in a country with no environmental legislation, but has a widely-published environmental policy (with a track record of honouring it) to clean up all contamination it causes.

  • Obligating event? A constructive obligation — no legal requirement, but the published policy + track record creates a valid expectation the company will act, leaving no realistic alternative to settling the obligation. Met.
  • Probable outflow? Yes.
  • Reliable estimate? Yes.
  • Conclusion: Provision recognized for the best estimate of clean-up costs.

Contingent assets — the asymmetry with contingent liabilities
  • IFRS: a contingent asset is disclosed (not recognized) when an inflow of economic benefits is probable. If virtually certain, it's no longer contingent — the related asset is recognized.
  • ASPE contingent gains: "not accrued in financial statements, as this accounting treatment could result in the recognition of revenue that might never be realized." Disclosed in notes only "when it is likely that a future event will confirm that an asset had been acquired or a liability reduced."
Exam angle — asymmetryBoth frameworks are deliberately conservative about gains vs. losses: a probable loss that's measurable gets recognized (a provision), but a probable gain only gets disclosed, never recognized, until it is virtually certain (IFRS) or realized (ASPE). This is the "prudence"/"conservatism" qualitative characteristic from Week 1 in action.
Real-world illustration — Cineplex & Bombardier

The lecture repeatedly walks the same three-criteria table (present obligation / probable outflow / reliable estimate) against actual disclosures in Cineplex's and Bombardier's annual reports across different years, showing how the same company's classification of an item can shift from "contingent liability" one year to "provision" the next as facts develop (e.g. a lawsuit's probability of loss increasing, or a settlement becoming more certain) — a good reminder that these classifications are reassessed every reporting period, not fixed at initial recognition.

Week 5

Subsequent Events & Accounting Changes

What to do about events discovered between year-end and the date financial statements are authorized for issue, and the three-way distinction between a change in estimate, a change in accounting policy, and a prior period error.

Subsequent events (IAS 10)

The "subsequent events period" runs from the reporting date to the date the financial statements are authorized for issue (a specific date IAS 10 requires companies to disclose).

Criteria — Adjusting event
1. The event occurs during the subsequent-events period, and
2. The event provides evidence of conditions that existed at the end of the reporting period

If both are met → adjust the financial statements. If the event relates to conditions that arose after year-end (not evidence of a year-end condition) → it's a non-adjusting event, disclosed in the notes only (if material) but the numbers themselves are not changed.

IAS 10 adjusting-event example"The receipt of information after the reporting period indicating that an asset was impaired at the end of the reporting period… For example: (i) the bankruptcy of a customer that occurs after the reporting period usually confirms that the customer was credit-impaired at the end of the reporting period; and (ii) the sale of inventories after the reporting period may give evidence about their net realisable value at the end of the reporting period."
Worked example — Capilano

Year-end is June 30, 20X1. In August, the audit team learns that 25% of year-end inventory was actually contaminated (unsellable) — the financial statements were not yet authorized for issue in October when this was flagged.

  • Occurs during subsequent-events period? Yes — information received in August, after year-end (June 30) and before statements were authorized for issue (still ongoing in October).
  • Evidence of a condition that existed at year-end? Yes — the contamination existed at June 30, it just wasn't discovered until later.
  • Conclusion: Adjusting event. Recommended entry: Dr. Inventory Write-down (expense) / Cr. Allowance for Unsellable Inventory.
Accounting changes (IAS 8) — three categories
CategoryDefinitionApplied
Change in estimateIFRS: monetary amounts subject to measurement uncertainty. ASPE: an adjustment to the carrying amount of an asset/liability (or its periodic consumption) resulting from reassessing the present status and expected future benefits/obligations. Arises from new information, new developments, or more experience.Prospectively (forward only — no restatement of prior periods)
Change in accounting policyA voluntary change to the specific principles/rules applied when preparing financial statements — permitted only when it provides more reliable and relevant information to users.Retrospectively (restate prior periods as if the new policy had always applied)
Prior period errorOmissions or misstatements from a failure to use, or misuse of, reliable information that was available and could reasonably have been expected to be obtained and used when those prior financial statements were prepared — includes math mistakes, misapplied policies, oversights, misinterpretation of facts, and fraud.Retrospectively (restate as if the error had never happened)
Memory aidEstimates look forward (prospective) because new information changed your best guess about the future. Policy changes and errors look backward (retrospective) because the correct treatment was knowable — either it's a deliberate switch to a better policy, or a mistake that should be fixed as if it never happened.
Worked example — Blue Sky Ltd. (both a change in estimate AND a prior period error)

1. Propane Burner Patent — Change in Estimate

Patent purchased Jan 1, 2023 for $2,525,000, originally expected 10-year useful life. Significant technology improvements occurred during 2025; Blue Sky will keep using the existing patent through 2026, then switch to new technology.

  • Patent amortization is a monetary amount in the F/S subject to measurement uncertainty (useful life estimate) → change in estimate criteria met.
  • New information (the 2025 technology developments, and management's now-concrete plan to stop using this patent after 2026) changes the estimate of remaining useful life.
  • Applied prospectively — the patent's remaining carrying amount (after 2023–2024 amortization) is now amortized over its revised remaining useful life (through end of 2026), with no restatement of 2023/2024.

2. Basket Design Patent — Prior Period Error

Another patent, purchased July 2, 2024 for $870,000, was expensed in error in 2024 (internal audit caught this during 2025) — it should have been capitalized. Expected useful life 10 years, no residual value, straight-line.

Financial Assets — Equity Investments

Strategic vs. non-strategic equity investments, why the level of influence you hold determines the entire accounting model, and how the new IFRS 18 affects where dividend income shows up.

Financial asset definition

A financial asset includes: cash; an equity instrument of another entity; a contractual right to receive cash or another financial asset from another entity; or a contractual right to exchange financial instruments on potentially favourable terms.

Strategic equity investments — extent of influence

Worked example — Robson Engineering Ltd. (Hydro One bonds)

Jan 1, 20X1: Robson buys $100,000 face value of Hydro One 8% bonds for cash. Market rate at purchase = 10%. Bonds mature Jan 1, 20X6 (5 years); interest paid semi-annually (4% per period, 8%/2), on July 1 and Jan 1. Held in a portfolio with the objective of holding to maturity to generate operating cash inflows.

  • Business model: "hold to collect" (held to maturity for operating cash flow) → supports Amortized Cost or FVOCI depending on whether selling is also part of the model.
  • Cash flow test: fixed coupon + principal at maturity = SPPI passes.
  • Because the coupon rate (8%) is below the market rate (10%) at purchase, the bond is acquired at a discount — initial cost is less than $100,000 face value, calculated as the present value of the cash flows (semi-annual $4,000 coupons + $100,000 at maturity) discounted at the market rate of 5% per semi-annual period.
  • Interest income each period is recognized using the effective interest rate method: Interest Income = Carrying amount × market rate (5% per period) — which is higher than the cash coupon received (4% × face value) while the bond trades at a discount, with the difference added to the bond's carrying amount (discount amortization) each period until it reaches face value at maturity.

IAS 16.39-40Revaluation increase → OCI, accumulated as Revaluation Surplus — unless it reverses a decrease on the same asset previously recognized in P&L (then P&L first). Intangible Assets & Goodwill

Purchased vs. internally-generated intangibles, the six-criteria test for capitalizing development costs, subsequent measurement, and why goodwill can never be internally generated.

Does it meet the definition of an intangible asset?

Same "Expenditures" sequence from Week 1: is it an Asset or Expense? An intangible asset is an identifiable non-monetary asset without physical substance. "Identifiable" means either separable (can be sold/transferred/licensed independently) or arising from contractual/legal rights.

Recognition (IFRS & ASPE, separate acquisition)Recognize when it's probable that expected future economic benefits will flow to the entity, and the cost can be measured reliably. For a separately purchased intangible, both criteria are normally considered automatically satisfied by the purchase transaction itself.
Subsequent measurement — amortization
  • Finite useful life → amortize over that life (straight-line unless another pattern better reflects consumption of benefits) — same mechanics as depreciation for tangible assets.
  • Indefinite useful life (no foreseeable limit to the period the asset is expected to generate cash flows — e.g. many trademarks) → not amortized; instead tested for impairment at least annually (see Week 12).
Internally generated intangibles — the Research vs. Development split
Research phase

Original, planned investigation undertaken to gain new scientific/technical knowledge. Always expensed as incurred — too uncertain to capitalize (IFRS & ASPE agree).

Development phase

Application of research findings to a plan/design for production of new/improved materials, products, processes, etc. Capitalized only if all 6 criteria below are met (IFRS — mandatory); ASPE offers a policy choice to expense or capitalize if its own criteria are met.

IAS 38 — the six development-phase recognition criteria (ALL must be met)

  1. Technical feasibility of completing the intangible asset so it will be available for use or sale
  2. Intention to complete the intangible asset and use or sell it
  3. Ability to use or sell the intangible asset
  4. How the intangible asset will generate probable future economic benefits
  5. Availability of adequate technical, financial and other resources to complete development and to use or sell it
  6. Ability to reliably measure the expenditure attributable to the intangible asset during its development
ASPE Pt II 3064.40–41Entities make an accounting policy choice to either expense development costs as incurred, or capitalize them if criteria similar to IFRS's six are met — this policy choice itself is a distinguishing feature vs. IFRS's mandatory capitalize-if-met rule.

Goodwill
GoodwillOnly recognized when acquired as part of a business combination (IFRS 3 / ASPE Pt II 3064) — the excess of the purchase price over the fair value of identifiable net assets acquired. Internally generated goodwill is never recognized as an asset under either framework, because its cost cannot be reliably distinguished from the cost of developing the business as a whole.

Subsequent measurement: goodwill is never amortized — it's tested for impairment at least annually (or more often if indicators exist), and impairment losses on goodwill are never reversed under IFRS (see Week 12).

PP&E — Cost Model vs. Revaluation Model (IAS 16)

The course's own "potential financial reporting issues" map lists "Cost Model vs. Revaluation Model (IFRS)" as a recurring subsequent-measurement issue alongside impairment — after initial recognition, IAS 16 lets an entity choose one of two models, applied to an entire class of PP&E (e.g. all land, or all buildings — not asset-by-asset):

ModelSubsequent measurement
Cost ModelCost less accumulated depreciation less accumulated impairment losses (the default/simplest model — the only one ASPE allows)
Revaluation ModelFair value at the date of revaluation, less any subsequent accumulated depreciation and impairment losses — provided fair value can be measured reliably. Must be revalued regularly enough that the carrying amount doesn't differ materially from fair value at each reporting date.

Where do revaluation gains and losses go?

MovementTreatment
Revaluation increaseRecognized in OCI, accumulated in equity as a Revaluation Surplus — unless it reverses a revaluation decrease of the same asset previously recognized in profit or loss, in which case the increase is recognized in P&L (income) up to the amount of that earlier decrease, with any excess to OCI.
Revaluation decreaseRecognized in profit or loss — unless there's an existing credit balance in that asset's Revaluation Surplus, in which case the decrease is debited against the surplus (through OCI) first, with any excess recognized in P&L.
Exam angle — the "first movement sets the rule" logicThink of each asset's Revaluation Surplus account as a bucket that absorbs good news and bad news symmetrically, in the order they actually occur: the first revaluation movement on a given asset follows the default rule (increase → OCI, decrease → P&L); every movement after that first one first tries to reverse/offset whatever is already sitting in that asset's own surplus account before spilling over to P&L or OCI.

Worked example

Equipment: original cost $500,000, accumulated depreciation to date $200,000, carrying amount $300,000. An independent appraisal values the equipment at $380,000.

Revaluation increase = 380,000 - 300,000 = $80,000 (assume no prior revaluation history for this asset - first movement) Using the "elimination" method (eliminate old accumulated depreciation against cost, then restate net amount to FV): Accumulated Depreciation - Equipment 200,000 Equipment (cost) 200,000 (eliminates the $200,000 A/D against the $500,000 gross cost, leaving Equipment at its $300,000 carrying amount) Equipment 80,000 Revaluation Surplus (OCI) 80,000 (restates the $300,000 carrying amount up to the $380,000 fair value) Result: Equipment carried at $380,000; Revaluation Surplus of $80,000 sits in equity (OCI), NOT in net income. Depreciation going forward is based on the new $380,000 amount.

An alternative "proportional" method restates both the gross cost and accumulated depreciation proportionately so the ratio between them is preserved while the net carrying amount still lands at fair value — either method is acceptable under IAS 16 as long as the resulting net carrying amount equals fair value.

ASPEASPE does not permit the revaluation model at all — PP&E under ASPE is always measured using the cost model (cost less accumulated depreciation less impairment), full stop. If a case states the entity applies ASPE, "Cost Model vs. Revaluation Model" is not even a live issue — go straight to the cost model.

Investment Property (IAS 40)
Investment property (IAS 40)Land or a building (or part of a building) held to earn rental income and/or for capital appreciation — rather than for use in producing/supplying goods or services or for administrative purposes (that's owner-occupied PP&E, IAS 16), and rather than for sale in the ordinary course of business (that's inventory, IAS 2).

The classification question itself is often the financial reporting issue: the same physical building can be PP&E, inventory, or investment property depending entirely on how and why the entity holds it — always tie your classification conclusion to specific case facts about the property's intended use.

Two measurement models — an accounting policy choice

ModelSubsequent measurement
Cost ModelSame as PP&E's cost model — cost less accumulated depreciation less impairment. Fair value must still be disclosed in the notes even if not used for measurement.

Unlike PP&E's revaluation model (applied class-by-class), the investment property measurement model, once chosen, is normally applied consistently to all of an entity's investment property.

Transfers between classificationsIf a property's use changes — e.g. an owner-occupied head office is vacated and leased out to third parties, or a rental building is repurposed for the entity's own operations — the property is reclassified between PP&E and investment property at that date, and IAS 40 sets out specific rules for measuring the property at the transfer date (generally at fair value, with the treatment of any resulting gain/loss depending on the direction of the transfer and which measurement model was in use). This is flagged here for awareness; work through the specific direction of transfer using case facts if it comes up.

  • STI's performance does not create an asset with an alternative use to STI — the panels are custom-designed for VRE's specific buildings, and STI does not sell its designs or resell panels to other customers.
  • STI has an enforceable right to payment for performance completed to date — evidenced by the contractual progress-billing schedule (initial billing partway through the project, before full completion).

Supervisor's question: recommend a different revenue recognition method?

The supervisor asked whether a different method should be used for performance obligations where control transfers over time. This is a genuine judgement call with reasonable arguments on both sides:

Case for keeping the input method
  • Costs are objectively tracked and readily available from STI's own accounting records — reliable and verifiable.
  • Consistent with the "hold to collect" logic used across the course's other long-term-contract examples (KTI/Polarian, Kennedy Construction).
Case for an output method instead
  • The case facts explicitly state: "Once the panels are installed, they are functional and VRE is able to benefit from them" — this directly ties customer benefit to installation milestones, not to cost inputs.
  • Phase 1 (design) ran 11% over budget in 2024 ($700,000 actual vs. $630,000 estimated) — under the input method, this cost overrun increases revenue recognized without VRE receiving any additional value, since design activity alone provides VRE no functional benefit.
  • An output method based on buildings/panels actually installed and functional would more faithfully depict the transfer of control to VRE, consistent with IFRS 15's core objective.
Recommended conclusionA reasonable recommendation is to consider an output method based on installed/functional capacity (e.g. % of buildings completed and energised) if that information can be obtained without undue cost — because it ties revenue recognition directly to the point the case says the customer actually benefits, and avoids the input method's exposure to distortion from cost inefficiency. The caveat: IFRS 15 permits output methods only when they can be applied without undue cost or effort, so this recommendation should be paired with a note that STI would need to confirm reliable, cost-effective tracking of installed capacity before switching methods.
